Placing the Blame

the guilt of becoming visible

I think I first understood something about myself when I was allowed to play someone else.

When I roleplayed as a boy, or acted in plays and got to keep the names written for those characters, something felt honest in a way I did not know how to explain. I could be loud, awkward, boyish, or ordinary without feeling like I was doing something wrong. Being feminine, on the other hand, often felt like wearing a part that did not fit me.

Coming out did not make everything simpler. My sister asked if I was really sure. Other questions followed: whether I wanted attention, whether I needed to pretend to be sick. I knew those questions might have come from confusion, but they still made me wonder if I had done something wrong by telling the truth.

For a while, I treated my own identity like a problem I had caused. I felt guilty that being honest about myself made things complicated for everyone else. I wondered whether I was supposed to be “man enough” to take the blame for making people uncomfortable, as if their discomfort meant I had done something cruel.

But I was not trying to hurt anyone. I was trying to explain myself.

They Will Bury Me a Woman

the fear of being forgotten

After a while, I stopped talking about being transgender as much.

I kept some things to myself. I started using “Sha,” a name that felt comfortably gender-neutral, and for a time I thought maybe that was enough. It was easier to leave parts of myself unspoken than to keep explaining something that other people did not seem ready to understand.

But there was a fear underneath that silence: what if nobody ever knew me as a man? What if the people around me kept the version of me they already understood, and that was the only version they would remember?

My sister thought I might be pretending. I had not told many other people. The name Shane belonged to the person I was trying to become, but Shalanie was the person everyone already knew how to talk about. I worried that even after a whole life, people might still remember the wrong person.

That fear was bigger than a name. It was the thought that people could love the memory they had of me and never really meet the person standing in front of them.

Goodbye, My Danish Sweetheart

the person people choose to remember

Eventually, I started reaching for help instead of trying to carry everything alone.

I talked to a guidance counselor and found online communities where other people understood what I was trying to say. I came out to friends. Some supported me, and that mattered. Others continued treating me the same way as before: using my old name and pronouns, or making feminine jokes around me as though nothing had changed.

One of my sisters told me she would miss doing my makeup now that I was a boy. She said she would try to learn my name, but that Shane sounded too weird for me, so maybe she would not learn it. I did not know how to explain how strange it felt to hear someone treat my name like an optional preference, as if respecting it was something she could simply decline.

They still had me right there. I had not disappeared. But sometimes it felt as though people preferred remembering the “old me,” the simpler version that was easier to explain to other people. Maybe it was more comfortable for them to keep that picture than to look at who I was becoming.

It made me wonder if this had ever been about what made me happy, or if it was about what looked familiar and acceptable to everyone else.

You’re a Big Boy Now

growing into a life of my own

There is a part of growing up that means accepting that some people will never get to see who you become.

My mother will never know me as Shane, or see the person I am growing into. I sometimes wonder what she would have thought, whether she would have understood, or whether she would have needed time. I will never know, and I think that is something I will always grieve.

For a long time, I thought becoming myself meant leaving someone behind. The little girl everyone knew, the name I grew up with, the version of me that my family could easily recognize. I felt guilty for changing, as if growing into someone happier meant taking something away from the people who loved me. As if I had to remain familiar to them to prove that I was still the same person they loved.

But I am still here. I still carry my childhood, my memories, and the people who shaped them. Becoming Shane does not erase any of that. I can miss the things I never got to experience with my mother and still look forward to the life I have ahead of me. I can acknowledge the child everyone remembers without forcing myself to remain that child forever.

Maybe that is what growing up means to me now. Not becoming the person everyone expected, but learning that I am allowed to become someone they did not expect, too.

I am a boy. I have been, even when I did not know how to explain it, even when I was afraid to say it aloud, and even when the people around me struggled to understand. I do not need everyone to understand me before I can accept myself.

So, to the little girl everyone remembers: you can rest now. You don’t have to keep pretending to be the person everyone thinks you to be. You have grown up, and you are allowed to grow into someone else.

I'm a big boy now. And even if not everyone gets to know me as one, I know who I am.
